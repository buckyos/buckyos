MomsAVoxell 6 minutes ago | prev | next [–]

Those tadpole shrimps are also known as Triops and they are a fairly common educational product/beginner pet sold in toy shops.

In the early days of the web I had a Triops tank set up with my SGI Indy camera pointed at it, and had visitors all over the world watching the Triops growing. It was awesome .. the kit came with a bunch of eggs, I just threw them all in there and watched as fairy shrimp, banana shrimp, and tadpole shrimp all hatched and went through their life cycles. I placed the tank under a desktop lamp, pointed the Indycam at it, and basically went hands-off for the 5 or 6 weeks that the whole thing was very entertaining.

The banana shrimp whooped up first, then the fairies and then the triops. It was really amusing .. the banana's dominated the cycle early, consuming all the food very fast, generally swimming around in circles just eating, eating .. and occasionally a fairy shrimp would get trapped in its talons, and we'd watch the banana shrimp consume a fairy or two .. while the baby Triops hatchlings mostly swam around the bottom surface, trolling for detritus and un-caught food pellets, growing growing in size by the day .. and they are cannibalistic little critters too, so there was a daily reckoning for the first 10 or 15 of them that hatched .. we (SGI Indy camera audience) would watch them slowly growing, occasionally catching each other .. until they got big enough to swim into the main middle section of the tank, and then .. the battle was ON!

Banana shrimp, almost a school of them, were whittled down over the course of a few days, the 30 Triops or so that formed went down to 5 or 6, we'd occasionally catch a fat Triops devouring a banana, and on one fun occasion, a banana being consumed by a Triops while also consuming a fairy .. it was a real Battle Royale of beasties.

Until, one fateful day, all there was left in the tank was one big, fat, very well fed Triops about the size of my thumb, swimming long circles around and around, all on its own.

I let the water evaporate and the sludge at the bottom dry, and pointed the SGI Indy camera at other things meanwhile, then a few weeks later, added more water to the dry dusty layer and filled the tank again. The whole cycle repeated itself - everyone had successfully reproduced! Bananas and fairies going at it in the early stages, only to be resolutely hunted by the Triops which, once again, whittled the entire tanks population down to one big, fat, well fed beastie, swimming its circuit.

Truly fun little critters. I wouldn't be surprised though, if some Florida kid introduced them to the pond, they can be somewhat traumatizing if you're not ready for cannibalism and stuff being eaten alive right in front of you ...

The tadpoles and dragonfly nymphs make me think of a question:

this metamorphic lifestyle is a way for one species to exploit two different ecological niches. The is more dramatic for the dragonflies than it is for the frogs - each dragonfly stage is incapable of living in the environment inhabited by the other stage.

So you have your dragonflies splitting their time between the water and the air in an environment that supports roughly X nymphs and Y adults.

Suppose there's a change to the environment and the air becomes a much more rewarding place relative to the water. The environment will now support X nymphs and 4Y adults. How does the species respond to the environmental change?

I can think of some options:

- No change; they're bottlenecked on X nymphs and X nymphs at any given time mean no more than Y adults. The extra resources in the air are abandoned to other more adaptable species.

- The nymph stage of the life cycle gets much shorter; a species that used to spend two years as a nymph now spends 6 months that way.

- The adult stage of the life cycle gets much longer.

- The nymph stage gets so much shorter that it's reduced to zero; the dragonflies throw off a new species with nonaquatic nymphs. Kind of a blend of the first two options.

The problem appears fairly general to the phenomenon of a single species that consumes different resources at different stages of its life. The availability of those resources will vary independently, and you want to be able to respond to changes, but the supply of organisms at each stage of life is much less independent. Is there a general theory of this kind of thing?

You’re missing the most obvious thing: dragonflies and nymphs are both predatory. This puts the nymphs at a significant disadvantage, to the point where they might never develop into adults.

Dragonflies do prey on a newly emerging nymph (a teneral) just as it climbs out of the water onto a reed or rock to shed its exoskeleton.

xfil 15 minutes ago | parent | prev | next [–]

I agree with part of your point about how nature-devoid urbanization should be avoided, and how extreme levels of density can cause problems, but I think you're presenting a false dichotomy.

Urban density can be used to preserve access to nature (or at least agrarian countryside) for large populations, while low-density sprawl is often a destructive force. The outcome is essentially always a result of policy decisions regarding land conservation, access infrastructure, nature education, law enforcement, and how residential & commercial development are structured.

The Randstad in the Netherlands (metropolitan area consisting of ~8.5 million people across Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and nearby cities) has roughly the same population as the Dallas-Forth Worth metroplex. The relatively higher density and lack of suburban sprawl amongst the Randstad's cities affords its population substantially easier access to quiet countryside than the DFW metroplex.
